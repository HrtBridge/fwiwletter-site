For What It's Worth - Week 1

September 23, 2026

Hey neighbor,

For what it's worth, I spoke with a dozen Andrew County residents this week, asking each of them the same question: "What is the biggest issue facing Andrew County today?"

I wasn't sure what I was going to get — my wife Nicole and I are new real estate agents, so we're used to people hearing our occupation and immediately becoming uncomfortable. However, of the 20 numbers I dialed, many picked up, a few called back, and everyone I spoke with was very interested in sharing their thoughts, even knowing full well what I do for a living. I was the editor of The Savannah Reporter for half a decade, so people know me as "the blue-haired Rosenauer kid" (no blue hair nowadays), and I was optimistic that some calls would be positive. I wasn't ready for how many locals would want to talk, some of them rather personally. After the first week, I'm realizing that our community is desperate to be heard — I only hope I can truly listen.

You didn't come here to read my rambling, so without further ado (and in full anonymity), here's what our neighbors said:

The first thing I heard was something I expected, but still wasn't ready for. People miss The Savannah Reporter newspaper the way it “used to be.” Five different conversations came back to the same thing: people feel like they lost a piece of county life. "Without The Reporter doing what it’s supposed to, we don't have much coverage of Andrew County anymore." They talked about school events, homecoming pictures, court news, investigations — the little things that told people what was happening around them, as well as the bigger stories that might've helped move the dial. One woman told me, "I get more off Facebook than I do from The Savannah Reporter." Another picked one up out of habit and came away wondering, "I'm thinking, why did I buy it?" Another told me she won't pay for it at all: "I refuse to pay for it because there's nothing in it." Another said she wishes someone would buy the paper and bring that community connection back. As a former owner with my dad Ken, I can tell you firsthand that the work to make a print newspaper happen is astounding, often thankless, but wildly rewarding — though this formerly blue-haired kid has no interest in owning a print newspaper ever again.

Then the conversation moved to something that has been around as long as the government has existed: the money to fund the government. One neighbor said the biggest problem isn't wanting better roads, schools, or services — it's whether people are willing to pay for them. "I'm as Republican as they come. But I vote yes on pretty much any tax because I believe that's how we pay for the fancy things we get." His point was simple: "The money's got to come from somewhere." Growing up in Savannah, I always considered myself in the same political vein as my grandma: an old-school Democrat, the kind who supported laborers and farmers but was down-to-earth about what they expected from the government. It's easy to forget that local Republicans and Democrats don't have to be anything like the larger, national parties.

Another neighbor took a more practical view: county roads "can always be better, but they've been worse." Then came another answer: "lack of motivation." That neighbor pointed to things around the county that feel neglected — overgrown areas, aging buildings, and roads that need attention. "You don't have to go anywhere other than down the poorly maintained road we have out here on 427 to see that it looks terrible."

The schools came up too. One neighbor who has spent time working in schools said she worries about student behavior and whether there are enough consequences. Her concern came down to one sentence: "there have to be repercussions."

"They're going to be taking care of me someday."

As the father of a brand-new baby boy, I appreciate the role I have in raising children with both discipline and love; though admittedly, I should probably take a class on both.

But that same neighbor also wanted to recognize something positive — the people who helped make the Andrew County Aquatics Initiative happen. "worked their butts off." "Sheer dedication and heart." I couldn't agree more — you'd have to have lived under a dump truck of rocks if you didn't see the incredible fundraising efforts behind the ACAI. But not everyone embraced the ACAI with open arms — a story for a different day.

One neighbor had an immediate answer when I asked the question: data centers. She said the threat of AI data centers has been "heavy on my heart" — she believes they could have "detrimental effects" on water, land, and crops, and she says developers are already circling nearby counties, and looking at Andrew County too. Another neighbor said they're watching the fight in nearby counties and worry it's coming here. No matter your opinion on data centers, surely we can all agree — the literal land under our feet here in Andrew County is special: it's where we build our homes, our schools, our livelihoods. A corporation from outside our community will likely never have the same respect for where we live; I can only hope our government officials are careful who they open the gates for.

Public safety came up from two different directions. One neighbor remembered questions around the fire department and insurance rates and said they'd like to better understand what happened. I had thought he was referring to a change in the Savannah ISO fire rating from a few years back; as it turns out, that ISO rating for insurance has changed once again. I won't pretend I know a dang thing about insurance, so if you want to learn more, call your local provider. Another worried about future funding for current maintenance and new projects: "At what cost? Because at some point you're always robbing Peter to pay Paul." But he was hopeful about one new revenue source — wind and solar development, pointing to nearby communities where turbines have brought new money to local school and fire districts. It's a tricky balance, data centers versus clean-energy sources; I'm glad I'm a real estate agent, not one of the folks deciding the future of our community for decades to come. Lord, bless the local government officials, for they too are only human.

Not everyone saw a county in trouble. Some people simply said they like living here. "Small-town warmth... good place to be and good people," said one caller who flat-out refused to answer the question of "issues in the county."

And truthfully, I may have made a mistake in making the very first question of the adventure so focused on the negative. Of course — who doesn't want to vent about all the bad they see around them? I, too, fall victim to that storm of negativity. You don't begin a career in journalism because you want to shed light on all the perfect, beautiful parts of a community.

However, in that very spirit of hopeful optimism comes our question for next week:

If you wanted to encourage a friend to move to Andrew County, what would you say to convince them?

New callers every week, so if you know who I should talk to next, have a recommendation for next week's question, or want to answer this new question, let me know! Feel free to share this newsletter with anyone who might be interested.

Until next week, stay busy.

Brent Rosenauer
